4. Stellar Properties and Approximate Relationships
The website provides approximate main-sequence relationships.
Let:
* MMM = stellar mass
* LLL = luminosity
* RRR = stellar radius
* TTT = stellar surface temperature
Values are expressed relative to the Sun.
A. Mass–luminosity relationship
LL⊙≈(MM⊙)3.5\frac{L}{L_{\odot}} \approx \left(\frac{M}{M_{\odot}}\right)^{3.5}L⊙​L​≈(M⊙​M​)3.5
This estimates how luminous a main-sequence star is based on its mass.
Example
For a star with half the Sun's mass:
M=0.5M⊙M = 0.5M_{\odot}M=0.5M⊙​
L≈0.53.5L \approx 0.5^{3.5}L≈0.53.5
This gives a rough luminosity estimate, not an exact real-world value.
B. Mass–radius relationship
RR⊙≈(MM⊙)0.8\frac{R}{R_{\odot}} \approx \left(\frac{M}{M_{\odot}}\right)^{0.8}R⊙​R​≈(M⊙​M​)0.8
This estimates stellar radius from stellar mass.
These relationships are approximations and may not accurately represent:
* Giant stars
* White dwarfs
* Neutron stars
* Evolved stars
* Very massive stars
* Stars outside the intended main-sequence range
Therefore, our README should specify that the model is intended for an approximate main-sequence star.
C. Temperature relationship
Using the Stefan–Boltzmann relationship:
T=T⊙(LR2)1/4T = T_{\odot} \left(\frac{L}{R^2}\right)^{1/4}T=T⊙​(R2L​)1/4
where the luminosity and radius are measured relative to the Sun.
This allows us to estimate the star's surface temperature.
Possible visual application
We can use the estimated temperature to determine the star's approximate colour:
* Cooler stars → reddish or orange
* Intermediate-temperature stars → yellowish or white
* Hotter stars → bluish-white
The colour should be treated as a visual approximation rather than a perfect physical rendering.

For a real-data project:

6. Habitable Zone
The approximate habitable-zone distance is given by:
rHZ≈LS AUr_{\text{HZ}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{L}{S}}\ \text{AU}rHZ​≈SL​​ AU
where:
* LLL = stellar luminosity relative to the Sun
* SSS = assumed stellar flux threshold
* rHZr_{\text{HZ}}rHZ​ = distance in astronomical units
For a simplified solar-flux assumption where S=1S=1S=1:
rHZ≈L AUr_{\text{HZ}} \approx \sqrt{L}\ \text{AU}rHZ​≈L​ AU
Meaning
The habitable zone is the region where conditions may allow liquid water under suitable atmospheric conditions.
It does not mean that a planet inside the zone is automatically habitable.
We should avoid claiming that our planets definitely contain life or liquid water unless we have additional atmospheric and environmental modelling.

8. Gravity and N-Body Simulation
The central physical principle is Newton's law of universal gravitation:
F=Gm1m2r2F = \frac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2}F=r2Gm1​m2​​
where:
* FFF = gravitational force
* GGG = gravitational constant
* m1,m2m_1,m_2m1​,m2​ = object masses
* rrr = distance between the objects
In a simulation, we generally calculate acceleration rather than directly moving objects using force.
a=Fma = \frac{F}{m}a=mF​
For an object attracted to another body:
a⃗=GMr3r⃗\vec{a} = \frac{GM}{r^3}\vec{r}a=r3GM​r
The exact vector implementation will depend on the coordinate system and direction used in the code.
N-body computation
An N-body simulation calculates the gravitational influence between multiple objects.
Examples:
* One star and several planets
* Two stars and planets
* A star, planets, and asteroids
* A spacecraft moving through a multi-body system
The naive approach has approximately:
O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2)
computational complexity because each object interacts with many other objects.
The number of unique object pairs is:
n(n−1)2\frac{n(n-1)}{2}2n(n−1)​
Practical guideline
For a browser-based project with a short deadline:
* Use a manageable number of bodies.
* Avoid creating thousands of physically simulated objects.
* Use visual-only particles if necessary.
* Keep the physics simulation separate from decorative effects.
9. Kepler's Law and Orbital Validation
The website asks us to test orbital behaviour using Kepler's law.
For a simplified two-body system using astronomical units, solar masses, and years:
P2≈a3MP^2 \approx \frac{a^3}{M}P2≈Ma3​
where:
* PPP = orbital period in years
* aaa = orbital semi-major axis in AU
* MMM = central mass in solar masses
For a planet orbiting a star with approximately one solar mass:
P2≈a3P^2 \approx a^3P2≈a3
Why this matters
We should not only make planets visually orbit. We should check whether the orbital period is approximately consistent with the expected relationship.
Possible validation panel:

The values above are only an example of the format. We should calculate actual values from our simulation.
10. Energy Drift
A simulation can look correct while gradually becoming physically unstable.
Energy drift measures how much the system's total energy changes over time.
A simplified mechanical energy calculation includes:
* Kinetic energy
* Gravitational potential energy
For a body:
KE=12mv2KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2KE=21​mv2
For two bodies:
PE=−Gm1m2rPE = -\frac{Gm_1m_2}{r}PE=−rGm1​m2​​
In a closed, idealized system, total energy should remain approximately constant. Numerical integration can introduce small errors.
What we should do
We do not need to create a highly advanced physics engine. However, we can:
* Track total energy at the beginning.
* Track total energy during the simulation.
* Display an approximate percentage drift.
* Mention the integration method and limitations.
For example:
Energy drift: 0.8%
Integrator: Velocity Verlet
Only display these values if they are actually calculated by our code.
11. Numerical Integration
The simulation needs a method to update position and velocity over time.
Possible approaches include:
Euler integration
* Easy to implement
* Simple to understand
* Can become unstable for orbital systems
* May produce significant energy drift
Semi-implicit Euler
* Still simple
* Often more stable than basic Euler
* Useful for a fast prototype
Velocity Verlet
* Better suited to many physics simulations
* Can provide improved energy behaviour
* Slightly more complicated to implement
Our strategy
We should choose an integration method that is:
1. Stable enough for our simulation.
2. Easy to explain.
3. Already tested.
4. Not unnecessarily complicated for the deadline.
We should not ask an AI tool to replace the entire physics engine repeatedly without first testing the existing implementation.
